## Before You Start: A Simple Analogy

Before we touch a single acronym, let's build intuition with something everyone already understands: **a restaurant kitchen.**

Imagine a busy restaurant:

- **The line cooks** prepare food every night. They follow the recipes, keep their stations clean, and are the first to notice if something is going wrong — a supplier delivered spoiled fish, or the fridge temperature is off. They are the ones actually *doing the work* and *managing the risk in front of them* in real time.
- **The kitchen manager / head chef** doesn't cook every dish personally, but sets the food-safety standards, trains the cooks, checks temperature logs periodically, and steps in if something looks wrong. They are not doing the cooking — they are making sure the cooking is being done *safely and correctly*, and they have the authority to challenge a cook who's cutting corners.
- **An independent health inspector** shows up unannounced, with no stake in how the kitchen "usually" does things, and checks whether food safety rules are actually being followed — not because they distrust the chef, but because an outside, independent check catches things that people too close to the daily routine might miss or excuse.
- **The restaurant owner (or a board, in a larger company)** isn't in the kitchen at all. They rely on reports from the manager and the inspector to know whether the restaurant is being run safely, and they're the ones ultimately answerable to customers and regulators if something goes wrong.

Hold onto this picture. As you read this room, you'll see that:

| Kitchen analogy | GRC term |
|---|---|
| Line cooks | **First Line** (operational management) |
| Kitchen manager / head chef | **Second Line** (risk & compliance functions) |
| Independent health inspector | **Third Line** (Internal Audit) |
| Restaurant owner / board | **Governing Body** |

This is exactly the logic behind the **Three Lines Model** — just applied to an entire organization instead of one kitchen. Keep this analogy in mind; we'll come back to it throughout the room whenever a concept feels abstract.

## Introduction

In Room 1, you learned that GRC is an integrated discipline built on three pillars — Governance, Risk, and Compliance. That room answered the question **"what needs to be managed?"** This room answers a different, equally important question: **"who is responsible for managing it, and who checks that they actually did?"**

Here's the honest truth about most real-world GRC failures: it's rarely because nobody thought of the risk. It's because **everyone assumed someone else owned it.** A policy existed. A risk was even listed in a spreadsheet somewhere. But no single person could be pointed to and asked, "did you make sure this was handled?"

This room focuses on the **Three Lines Model**, the most widely adopted framework for organizing accountability across an enterprise, and on the specific roles — from the Board down to individual control owners — that make a GRC program function day to day. By the end, you should be able to look at *any* task in an organization and say exactly which "line" is responsible for it, and why.

## Why Roles and Accountability Matter

Let's make this concrete with a short story.

Two companies both keep a risk register (a document listing known risks) that includes the same entry: *"Our vendor has broad access to our customer database, and that access has never been formally reviewed."*

**Company A:**
The entry sits in a spreadsheet. It was added a year ago by someone who has since left the company. Nobody's name is attached to it as the person responsible for fixing it. Nobody checks on it. It's technically "documented" — but documentation without an owner is just a to-do list nobody reads.

**Company B:**
The same entry has a **named risk owner** in the business unit that uses the vendor. A **second-line risk analyst** reviews that risk every quarter and pushes back when the assessment looks too optimistic. An **internal auditor** periodically tests whether the review is actually happening as described, not just on paper.

Now imagine the vendor is breached in both companies.

- In **Company A**, the breach investigation reveals the risk was known for over a year and nobody acted. This looks terrible to regulators and customers — the organization "knew and did nothing."
- In **Company B**, the risk was already flagged as overdue for remediation *before* the breach happened, and it had already been escalated to a risk committee. The organization can show it was actively managing the issue, even though the breach still occurred.

**The framework — a risk register — was identical in both companies.** The only difference was *who was accountable, and who checked that accountability was real.* That difference is exactly what the Three Lines Model exists to create.

> **In plain terms:** A list of risks is not a risk management program. A list of risks *with a named, accountable person behind each one, and someone independently checking that person's work* — that's a program.

## The Three Lines Model — Overview

The **Three Lines Model** is maintained by the **Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA)** — think of the IIA as a global professional body that sets standards for how internal audit and risk oversight should work, similar to how a medical board sets standards for doctors.

The model's whole purpose is simple: **make sure no single group is both doing a job and being the only judge of whether it was done well.** That's it. Everything else in this room is detail on top of that one idea.

It splits accountability into four layers:

| Layer | Who (using our kitchen analogy) | Core Question They Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Governing Body** | The restaurant owner / the Board | "Is management running things the way we expect, and can they prove it?" |
| **First Line** | The line cooks / operational staff | "Are we doing our jobs well, and managing the risks that are directly in front of us?" |
| **Second Line** | The kitchen manager / risk & compliance teams | "Are the first line's practices actually good enough — and am I willing to challenge them if not?" |
| **Third Line** | The independent health inspector / Internal Audit | "Can I, as someone with no stake in the daily routine, confirm that everything above is actually working?" |

A simple way to remember it: **the first line owns and does the work. The second line sets the standard and watches over it. The third line independently double-checks that the first two are actually doing what they claim.**

Let's now walk through each layer slowly, one at a time, with fresh examples for each.

---

## The Governing Body

The **Governing Body** sits above all three lines — it is typically the **Board of Directors**, or a relevant board committee such as the **Audit Committee** or **Risk Committee**. Think of the Governing Body as the group that is ultimately answerable to the outside world — shareholders, regulators, customers — if something goes badly wrong.

Importantly, the Governing Body **does not manage risk hands-on.** Going back to our analogy: the restaurant owner doesn't personally check the fridge temperature every night. But the owner *does* need to know, with confidence, that someone reliable is checking it — and that if that person says everything's fine, it's actually true.

### What the Governing Body Actually Does
- Sets the organization's overall strategy and decides how much risk it's willing to accept (its "risk appetite")
- Makes sure proper structures exist for governance, risk management, and control — i.e., makes sure a "kitchen manager" and an "inspector" actually exist and have real authority
- Holds management accountable when things go wrong
- Makes sure Internal Audit is independent and has the resources it needs to do its job properly
- Talks directly with outside parties like external auditors and regulators when needed

> **In plain terms:** The Governing Body doesn't do the checking. It makes sure the checking happens, is done properly, and is reported to them honestly.

---

## The First Line: Operational Management

The first line is made up of the people who **actually do the work** — and, importantly, who **own the risk that comes with doing that work.** This is the biggest group of people in any organization, because it includes every business unit and every employee performing day-to-day tasks.

### What "Owning the Risk" Actually Means

This is the single most misunderstood idea in this whole topic, so let's slow down here.

If a customer support employee has access to sensitive customer data, **the customer support team — not the security department — owns the risk of that data being mishandled.** The security team can write a policy, run training, and monitor compliance, but they cannot personally prevent every individual employee action. The team doing the actual work is the one closest to the risk, and therefore the one who must manage it day to day.

### Core Responsibilities
- Spotting and managing risks that come directly from their own daily work
- Building and running controls exactly where the risk actually happens — not somewhere else on paper
- Keeping normal business processes safe and correct (a finance clerk double-checking an invoice before payment, an IT technician applying a security patch on schedule)
- Telling the second line when something crosses a line they're not equipped to handle alone

### Worked Example
A customer support team handles personal customer data every single day. In our kitchen analogy, they're the line cooks working directly with the food. They are responsible for:
- Following the data-handling procedure the second line wrote for them
- Immediately reporting anything that looks like a data exposure — not waiting to see if it "blows over"
- Making sure their own daily habits (locking screens, not emailing files insecurely) match the privacy policy, even though they didn't write that policy themselves

> **In plain terms:** If a risk lives inside your daily job, it's your job to manage it — even if someone else wrote the rulebook you're following.

---

## The Second Line: Risk, Compliance, and Oversight Functions

The second line exists to give the first line **the tools, the standards, and the challenge** they need to manage risk well. This is where most dedicated "GRC" job titles actually live — risk management, compliance, information security governance, legal.

Think back to the kitchen manager. They don't cook every dish, but they:
- Write the food-safety rules the cooks follow
- Train new cooks on those rules
- Walk through the kitchen and check whether the rules are actually being followed
- Push back — politely but firmly — if a cook says "it's fine, I always skip that step and nothing bad has happened"

That last point is the part people miss most often: **the second line's job includes disagreeing with the first line when necessary.** That's not being difficult — it's the entire point of the role.

### Core Responsibilities
- Building the risk management framework, policies, and risk-scoring methods the first line uses
- Giving the first line practical tools and training so they can actually follow the rules, not just be told to
- Watching whether the first line's risk and control work is genuinely good enough
- Rolling up risk information from across the organization and reporting it upward
- Formally challenging a first-line risk assessment that looks too optimistic or incomplete

### Worked Example
An information security team (second line) writes the access control policy for the whole company. Every quarter, they check whether departments are actually complying with it — not just assuming they are because a policy exists. If the finance department rates a new vendor's risk as "Low" when the second line's own data suggests it should be "High," the second line has both the authority and the responsibility to push back before that rating is accepted anywhere else in the system.

> **In plain terms:** The second line rarely does the task itself. It sets the bar, watches whether the bar is being met, and speaks up loudly when it isn't.

---

## The Third Line: Internal Audit

The third line is **Internal Audit** — the independent health inspector in our analogy. Their entire value comes from one thing: they have **no personal stake** in whether the first or second line's work looks good. That distance is what makes their opinion trustworthy.

### Core Responsibilities
- Independently testing whether controls, processes, and risk management are actually working — not just documented
- Checking whether the first and second lines are genuinely doing their jobs, not just claiming to
- Reporting findings straight to the Governing Body (usually the Audit Committee) — deliberately bypassing the management being reviewed, to protect honesty
- Recommending fixes and following up later to confirm they actually happened

### Why Independence Is Non-Negotiable

Imagine if the restaurant's health inspector was hired and paid directly by the head chef, and reported their findings only to that same chef. Would you trust their report? Probably not — even if the inspector was personally honest, the *setup* itself creates pressure to go easy.

This is exactly why Internal Audit typically reports to the **Audit Committee of the Board**, not to the CFO, CISO, or any manager whose area they're reviewing. Reporting to the people you're auditing quietly destroys the value of the audit, even if no one intends it to.

### Worked Example
Internal Audit pulls a random sample of vendor risk assessments completed by the second line over the past year. They independently re-check: was the risk rating reasonable? Were the right approvals actually obtained? If something was flagged as "overdue," was it genuinely escalated the way policy required — or did that step get quietly skipped?

> **In plain terms:** The third line's entire job is to answer one question honestly, without fear of upsetting anyone: "is what everyone is telling the Board actually true?"

## External Assurance Providers

Outside the three internal lines, there's often one more layer of independent checking — this time from *outside* the organization entirely:

- **External auditors** — independent firms that check financial statements, and often internal controls too (for example, under laws like SOX)
- **Regulators** — government bodies that inspect whether specific laws are being followed (for example, a data protection authority investigating a company's GDPR compliance)
- **Certification bodies** — organizations that formally audit and certify a company against a standard, such as ISO 27001 or SOC 2

Back to the kitchen: this is like a **government food safety agency** occasionally showing up to check the restaurant independently of the owner's own inspector. It adds one more layer of trust, but it doesn't replace the internal ones — the restaurant still needs its own manager and inspector every single day, not just when the government happens to visit.

---

## Putting the Whole Picture Together

Here's the full picture in one place, now that you've seen every layer individually:

1. **First line** does the work and owns the risk that comes with it.
2. **Second line** sets the standards, gives support, and independently challenges the first line when needed.
3. **Third line** independently checks whether the first and second lines are genuinely doing what they say.
4. **Governing Body** relies on honest reporting from all of the above to fulfill its ultimate accountability to shareholders, regulators, and the public.
5. **External assurance providers** add one further outside check, on top of all internal layers.

If you can explain this five-step chain in your own words — ideally using the kitchen analogy — you've understood the core idea of this room. Everything from here on is detail and application.

## Key GRC Roles in Detail

Now that you understand *why* the lines exist, here's *who* actually fills each one in a typical organization.

| Role | Line | Primary Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Board of Directors** | Governing Body | Ultimate accountability for strategy, oversight, and integrity |
| **Audit Committee** | Governing Body | Oversees financial reporting, internal audit, and the external audit relationship |
| **Risk Committee** | Governing Body | Oversees enterprise risk appetite and major risk exposures |
| **CEO** | First Line (senior) | Overall accountability for achieving objectives within approved risk appetite |
| **Business Unit Managers** | First Line | Manage risk and controls within their own operational area |
| **Risk Owner** | First Line | The specific individual accountable for a specific identified risk and its treatment |
| **Control Owner** | First Line | The specific individual accountable for making sure one specific control works correctly |
| **Chief Risk Officer (CRO)** | Second Line | Leads the enterprise risk management framework and reporting |
| **Chief Compliance Officer (CCO)** | Second Line | Leads the compliance program, regulatory monitoring, and policy interpretation |
| **Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)** | Second Line (usually) | Leads information security strategy, policy, and risk oversight |
| **GRC Analyst / GRC Manager** | Second Line | Maintains the risk register, coordinates assessments, produces reporting |
| **Legal Counsel** | Second Line | Advises on regulatory obligations and legal risk |
| **Chief Audit Executive (CAE)** | Third Line | Leads the Internal Audit function; reports to the Audit Committee |
| **Internal Auditor** | Third Line | Performs independent testing and assurance work |
| **External Auditor** | External | Provides independent assurance to shareholders and regulators |

> **Don't panic if your organization doesn't have all these exact titles.** In a small company, one person might handle several second-line responsibilities at once (say, one GRC manager covering both risk *and* compliance). That's completely normal. What matters is not the job title — it's whether the *function* of independent challenge exists somewhere, for someone to actually perform, even informally.

## RACI Matrix — Applying It to GRC

A **RACI matrix** is one of the most useful, practical tools you'll use in GRC work, and it's much simpler than it first looks. RACI stands for:

- **R — Responsible:** the person(s) who actually *do* the work
- **A — Accountable:** the *one* person ultimately answerable if it goes wrong (there should only ever be one "A" per activity — more than one Accountable person usually means nobody really feels accountable)
- **C — Consulted:** people who are asked for input *before* a decision, but don't make the final call
- **I — Informed:** people who are simply told the outcome *after* the fact

### How to Read the Table Below

Take the first row, "Identify operational risk." It shows **R, A** under the First Line column. That means: the first line both *does* the work of identifying the risk **and** is the one held *accountable* for having done it properly. The second line is only **C** (Consulted) — they might offer guidance, but they're not the ones on the hook if it's missed.

| Activity | First Line (Business Unit) | Second Line (Risk/Compliance) | Third Line (Internal Audit) | Board |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identify operational risk | **R, A** | C | I | I |
| Set risk management framework | C | **R, A** | I | I |
| Approve enterprise risk appetite | I | C | I | **R, A** |
| Perform control testing (routine) | **R** | **A** | I | I |
| Independent control testing | I | I | **R, A** | I |
| Approve remediation plan | **R** | **A** | C | I |
| Report risk posture to Board | I | **R** | **R** | **A** |

Notice the row "Perform control testing (routine)": here Responsible and Accountable are *split* — the first line does the actual testing (R), but the second line is answerable for confirming it was done well (A). That split is intentional and is exactly the kind of detail a RACI is good at capturing.

**Try it yourself:** before moving on, cover the table and try to guess, from memory, who should be "R" and who should be "A" for *"Approve enterprise risk appetite."* Then check your answer against the table. If you got it right, you've understood the model. If not, re-read "The Governing Body" section above — the answer is there.

Building a RACI matrix like this for your own organization is one of the fastest, most practical ways to expose where accountability is currently unclear or missing entirely.

## From "Three Lines of Defense" to "Three Lines Model" — What Changed

You may come across an older name for this same framework: the **"Three Lines of Defense."** In 2020, the IIA renamed and refined it to the **"Three Lines Model."** It's the same underlying idea — don't worry that you're learning an outdated version — but a few things about how it's *framed* changed:

| Old Model (Three Lines of Defense) | Updated Model (Three Lines Model) |
|---|---|
| Framed purely around defending against threats | Framed around achieving objectives and creating value, not only defense |
| Suggested a rigid, strictly separated hierarchy | Emphasizes collaboration and alignment between lines, not just separation |
| Focused narrowly on risk and control | Explicitly includes governance and the Governing Body as its own distinct layer |
| Roles described fairly mechanically | Roles described as flexible principles, so they fit organizations of very different sizes and shapes |

**Why this matters to you practically:** if you read older material — a textbook, an older certification guide, an older company policy — and it says "Three Lines of Defense," you can mentally substitute "Three Lines Model." They're describing the same structure; the newer name just better reflects that the model is about *enabling good outcomes*, not just *blocking bad ones*.

## Common Mistakes and Role Confusion

These are the mistakes that show up again and again in real organizations. Read each one slowly — chances are you'll recognize at least one from a workplace you've seen or heard about.

- **Treating the second line as the risk owner.** It's tempting to think "the compliance team is responsible for data privacy risk" — but that's backwards. The compliance team sets the policy and watches over it; the business unit actually handling the data still owns the risk. This mistake quietly lets the first line stop paying attention, because "compliance has it handled."

- **Letting Internal Audit test its own work.** If the second-line risk team also performs the "independent" testing of their own controls, that testing isn't really independent anymore — no matter how honest the individuals are. True independence has to come from the third line, or from an outside party.

- **Skipping the second line under resource pressure.** In smaller or fast-growing organizations, it's common for the first line to just self-assess with nobody challenging them at all. This quietly removes the exact check the whole model exists to provide — and it's often only discovered after something goes wrong.

- **Internal Audit reporting to the people it audits.** If the Chief Audit Executive reports mainly to the CFO instead of the Audit Committee, independence is already compromised — even if every individual audit finding is technically accurate.

- **Confusing "Consulted" with "Accountable" in a RACI.** Being asked for your opinion (C) feels similar to being responsible for the outcome (A), but they are very different in practice. This mix-up is one of the single most common causes of a risk quietly having "nobody" actually accountable for it.

## Case Study: A Line of Defense Breakdown

**Background:** A retail company suffered a significant fraud loss when an employee in the finance department was able to both *initiate* and *approve* wire transfers for over a year, with nobody else checking.

**Root Cause Analysis — walking through it one line at a time:**

- **First line failure:** The finance team never flagged that one employee held two conflicting responsibilities at once (this is called a "segregation of duties" failure — one person shouldn't be able to both start *and* approve the same payment).
- **Second line failure:** A policy existed requiring segregation of duties — but the risk and compliance function never actually checked whether it was being followed in real life. The policy existed only on paper.
- **Third line failure:** Internal Audit hadn't tested wire transfer approval controls in over two years. The moment they finally did, they spotted the gap almost immediately.
- **Governing Body gap:** The Audit Committee never asked *when* wire transfer controls had last been tested, and nobody had flagged to them that this area was overdue for review.

**What this teaches you:** notice that *every single line existed on the org chart.* There was a finance team, a compliance function, an internal audit department, and a board committee. The failure wasn't a missing box on a diagram — it was that each box had quietly stopped doing its actual job.

**Remediation — how they fixed it, mapped back to each line:**
1. Built segregation of duties directly into the finance system so it's technically impossible for one person to do both steps (fixes the **first line**)
2. Added a quarterly self-certification check for segregation-of-duties compliance (strengthens **second-line** monitoring)
3. Increased how often high-risk financial controls get tested (strengthens the **third line**)
4. Required the Audit Committee to review audit coverage gaps every year, so this kind of blind spot gets caught sooner next time (strengthens **Governing Body** oversight)

**Lesson:** The Three Lines Model only creates value when each line is genuinely active — not just present as a name on an org chart. A framework that exists only on paper protects nobody.

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Three Lines Model** | IIA framework organizing accountability for risk and control across a Governing Body and three lines |
| **Governing Body** | The Board or board committee ultimately accountable to stakeholders for organizational oversight |
| **First Line** | Operational management that owns and manages risk directly, as part of doing its normal work |
| **Second Line** | Functions (risk, compliance, security) that set standards, support, and monitor first-line risk management |
| **Third Line** | Internal Audit — providing independent assurance to the Governing Body |
| **Independence** | Freedom from conditions that threaten objectivity — especially never reviewing your own work |
| **Risk Owner** | The one individual accountable for managing a specific identified risk |
| **Control Owner** | The one individual accountable for a specific control's effective operation |
| **Chief Audit Executive (CAE)** | The most senior Internal Audit leader, typically reporting to the Audit Committee |
| **RACI Matrix** | A tool mapping who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for a given activity |
| **Segregation of Duties (SoD)** | Ensuring no single person controls every step of a critical process, reducing fraud/error risk |
| **Self-Assessment** | A first-line practice of evaluating one's own risk and control status, which the second line should independently challenge |

## Summary

- Strong GRC programs depend as much on **clear accountability** as on the frameworks and policies themselves — a documented risk with no real owner protects nobody.
- The **Three Lines Model** organizes accountability into a Governing Body plus three lines: the **first line** (does the work, owns the risk), the **second line** (sets standards, supports, and challenges), and the **third line** (independently checks that both are genuinely working).
- **External assurance providers** — auditors, regulators, certification bodies — add a further independent layer from outside the organization, but they don't replace the three internal lines.
- Internal Audit's entire value rests on its **independence**, especially reporting to the Audit Committee rather than to the management it reviews.
- A **RACI matrix** turns abstract role definitions into something concrete and checkable for any specific GRC activity.
- The 2020 rename from "Three Lines of Defense" to "Three Lines Model" reframed the model around collaboration and creating value, not just blocking threats — the underlying structure stayed the same.
- Almost every real-world GRC failure traces back not to a missing policy, but to a line of accountability that quietly stopped functioning while still looking fine on paper.
